# 4. Results

## 4.1 Evaluation setup

Four forecasters are compared: the baseline probabilistic forecaster, a CRPS-only control
(baseline weights fine-tuned on pinball loss alone, at an identical training budget to the
decision-focused arms), and two decision-focused arms retrained under single-price and
dual-price settlement respectively. All are evaluated through the recourse-aware robust
dispatch formulation, based on [@stratigakosPrescriptiveTreesIntegrated2022], using affine
decision rules [@georghiouDecisionRuleApproach2019] for second-stage recourse, over the
full 366-day sealed test year for the balanced battery archetype (2 MW charge/discharge,
4 MWh capacity). The baseline and control are price-agnostic and are evaluated natively
under both settlement structures; each decision-focused arm is evaluated natively under
the structure it was trained against and cross-evaluated under the other. Each retrained
arm is trained at five seeds; the baseline is a single fixed checkpoint all retraining
warm-starts from and is not seed-averaged. Results throughout this chapter are reported as
mean ± standard deviation across seeds, the seed-wise range, win rate against baseline,
and, where relevant, sign consistency across all five seeds.

## 4.2 Forecast behaviour

Figure 4.1 shows the mean-centred forecast bias (predicted mean minus realised
prosumption) by hour of day, baseline against each retrained arm, seed-averaged with the
inter-seed spread shaded. The baseline forecaster carries a persistent positive bias at
every hour (over-predicting prosumption throughout the day), but the correction introduced
by retraining is concentrated in daytime hours: from roughly hour 6 to hour 20, and most
steeply between hours 8 and 17, all three retrained arms sit measurably below baseline.
Outside this window — overnight, hours 0–5 and 21–23 — the retrained arms sit at or
slightly above the baseline curve rather than below it, so the correction is not a uniform
shift but one localised to the period in which solar generation is active.

Within the daytime window, the three arms do not correct the bias identically. Across the
midday hours (11–16), the single-price arm sits closest to zero bias at five of the six
hours (11, 13, 14, 15, and 16); the control is closest only at hour 12, and only narrowly.
The single-price arm's path to that result is uneven rather than monotonic: it crosses
into negative bias at the shoulders of the midday window (hours 11–12 and 16) but reverts
to a positive bias comparable to the control's through the core of the window (hours
13–15). The dual-price arm shows a shallower version of the same non-monotonicity — a dip
towards zero at hour 12 that partially rebounds by hour 14, rather than approaching zero
and remaining there.

The inter-seed spread is not uniform across arms. At every hour in the midday window, the
single-price arm's seed-to-seed standard deviation is the largest of the three (0.054–0.098
MW), the dual-price arm's is the smallest (0.033–0.042 MW), and the control sits between
the two (0.053–0.079 MW). At several hours the single-price band straddles zero entirely
(for example, hour 11: −0.010 ± 0.093 MW; hour 16: −0.002 ± 0.054 MW), meaning individual
seeds land on either side of zero bias rather than converging on a shared value.

Figure 4.2 shows per-hour mean absolute error for each arm against baseline; all three
retrained arms sit close to the baseline curve, and the diurnal accuracy pattern is
essentially unchanged by retraining. Mean CRPS by forecaster is reported alongside — as
CRPS is computed directly from each forecaster's own raw quantiles against realised
prosumption, it is a fixed property of the checkpoint and is identical regardless of which
settlement pipeline that checkpoint is subsequently evaluated under. The mechanism
connecting these forecast-shape observations to the settlement structures' underlying cost
geometry is developed in §5.2.

In both tables, the arm trained under single-price settlement outperforms its table's
control, whether evaluated natively (Table 4.1: −0.126% against the control's −0.104%) or
cross-evaluated (Table 4.2: −0.127% against −0.096%). The arm trained under dual-price

appear as the native or the cross-evaluated arm — a pattern examined further in §5.4.

Sign consistency across the five seeds separates the eight forecaster-direction
combinations cleanly. Every row involving single-price training — the single-price
control, the single-price arm evaluated natively, and the single-price arm cross-evaluated
under dual-price settlement — is beneficial in all five seeds. Every row involving
dual-price training — the dual-price control, the dual-price arm evaluated natively, and
the dual-price arm cross-evaluated under single-price settlement — includes at least one
seed at or fractionally above zero, and so is not unanimous. This split does not track
whether an arm is native or cross-evaluated in a given table; it tracks only which
settlement structure trained the forecaster.

## 4.4 Robustness

The magnitude of the seed-to-seed spread reported throughout this chapter sets the
practical resolution limit of this evaluation design: differences smaller than roughly
0.05–0.10 percentage points, the typical standard deviation across the reported rows,
cannot be distinguished from noise at this seed count. One-sample t-tests against zero are
reported for the four decision-focused rows for which they were computed: DFL single-price,
evaluated natively (t = −2.96, p = 0.042) and cross-evaluated under dual-price settlement
(t = −2.85, p = 0.046); DFL dual-price, evaluated natively (t = −2.19, p = 0.094) and
cross-evaluated under single-price settlement (t = −3.50, p = 0.025). With n = 5, these
tests are individually weak, and the unanimous-sign rows identified in §4.3 constitute
stronger evidence than any single p-value — a result that is one-directional across five
independently-trained seeds is harder to produce by chance than a marginal test statistic
suggests. A direct comparison between the single-stage training formulation's own decision
value and its value under the recourse-aware robust formulation used throughout this
chapter was not built as part of this evaluation; this gap is treated as a stated
limitation in Chapter 6 rather than left implicit.

# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 What each settlement structure rewards

The two settlement structures differ in the geometry of the cost they impose on forecast
error. Under single-price settlement, imbalance cost is exactly linear in the committed
position: a single price applies regardless of whether the participant is long or short, so
the expected cost is minimised, in a risk-neutral sense, not by matching any particular
statistic of the forecast distribution but by a corner solution driven entirely by the
sign of the expected price spread [@stratigakosPrescriptiveTreesIntegrated2022] — the
settlement itself supplies no pull towards accuracy. Under dual-price settlement, by
contrast, the imbalance cost is a piecewise-linear, asymmetric hinge, kinked at the point
of zero deviation, with a genuine minimum at a price-implied quantile
τ = (π_up − π_da)/(π_up − π_down). On the GB market data used in this study, that quantile
is close to the median: τ = 0.43 on the training period and τ = 0.50 on the sealed test
year. This asymmetry is itself a documented property of the two market designs, not an
artefact of this study's formulation — deviations that help restore overall system balance
can be financially rewarded under single-price settlement, while dual-price settlement is
constructed specifically so that no participant can profit from deviating from its
committed position, regardless of direction. The single-price arm therefore has no
settlement-supplied minimum to converge towards during decision-focused retraining; the
dual-price arm does, and that minimum sits close to the ordinary statistical target that
CRPS-based training already pursues.

This geometry, derived from the settlement formulae rather than fitted to any observed
result, generates two testable predictions. A dual-price-trained forecaster should show
forecast behaviour converging towards its price-implied quantile with the seed-to-seed
consistency a genuine minimum permits; a single-price-trained forecaster should show no
such convergence, since nothing in its objective distinguishes one point along the
cost-indifferent direction from another. §5.2 tests both predictions directly against the
forecast-behaviour results in §4.2.

## 5.2 Do the observed changes match?

The forecast-behaviour results in §4.2 are broadly consistent with this structural
account, though not in the clean form the theory alone would suggest. The dual-price arm's
bias correction stalls partway towards zero and partially rebounds rather than converging
monotonically on its price-implied minimum — consistent with a real, if imperfectly
realised, pull towards a specific target. The single-price arm's correction is uneven:
it overshoots into negative bias at the shoulders of the midday window while
under-correcting through its core, and, more tellingly, its seeds disagree with one
another at every point across that window. This is the behaviour predicted by a cost
function with no minimum to converge to — different seeds settle at different points along
a direction with no anchor to arrest them, rather than converging on a shared value the way
a well-posed target would produce. The fact that the single-price arm's seed-to-seed
standard deviation is the largest of the three retrained arms at every hour in the midday
window, and that its band straddles zero at several of those hours, is the same structural
fact showing up as instability rather than as a directional bias — not two separate
observations but one. The single-price arm's accuracy improvement over baseline, where it
occurs, is attributable to the pinball-loss term that remains part of its combined training
objective throughout, not to the decision-focused term, which supplies no accuracy optimum
of its own to pull towards.

A further check comes from reconciling §4.2's two figures against one another. The bias
shift documented above is substantial at several hours (up to roughly 0.1–0.2 MW), yet
Figure 4.2's mean absolute error remains close to baseline across the same hours, with the
diurnal accuracy pattern essentially unchanged. A location shift of this size without a
corresponding improvement in per-measurement accuracy is consistent with retraining moving
where the forecast sits relative to realised prosumption without materially sharpening the
underlying predictive distribution — a location correction layered on similar dispersion,
rather than a genuinely more accurate forecast in the CRPS sense. This is also consistent
with §4.3's economic results: the same instability that separates the single-price arm
from the other two in the bias figure reappears in the economic seed spread. Both rows
involving single-price training carry a standard deviation of 0.085–0.089 percentage
points, against 0.043–0.046 for both rows involving dual-price training, regardless of
whether either is the native or the cross-evaluated arm in its table. The same underlying
mechanism is visible in both the forecast-shape data and the downstream economic data, not
two independent pieces of evidence.

## 5.3 Why decision value barely moved

Table 4.1 and Table 4.2 show that neither decision-focused arm produces an economically
distinguishable improvement over its matched, task-loss-free control. This follows
directly from the geometry established in §5.1. With the day-ahead bid tied to the forecast
and true settlement prices unknown at gate closure, the decision that minimises expected
cost is, for a risk-neutral participant, close to what conventional statistical training
already produces whenever the price-implied quantile is close to the median — which, on
this dataset, it is (τ = 0.50 on the test year). Cameron et al.'s result that decision-focused
training can outperform statistical training whenever the downstream objective is
non-linear in the prediction target [@cameronPerilsLearningOptimizing2021] establishes
non-linearity as a necessary condition for a genuine two-stage gap to exist, but this
dataset shows it is not a sufficient one: what determines whether decision-focused
retraining has room to improve on statistical training is not simply whether the objective
is non-linear, but whether the decision-optimal statistic implied by that non-linearity
actually differs from the statistic ordinary training already targets. Under GB dual-price
settlement, on this dataset, it does not differ meaningfully, and the near-identical
performance of the dual-price decision-focused arm and its CRPS-only control in Table 4.2
(−0.045% against −0.096%, both well within one seed-to-seed standard deviation of the
other) is the direct empirical consequence.

The single-price arm's economic value relative to its own control (§4.3: −0.126% against
−0.104% natively, −0.127% against −0.096% cross-evaluated) is not counter-evidence to this
account, and should not be read as a demonstrated single-price decision-focused benefit.
Single-price's decision-loss gradient with respect to the forecast mean has no minimum in
isolation, but the training objective actually optimised is a combination of that term with
the ordinary pinball loss, weighted so that whichever term is larger in a given batch
receives proportionally less weight on its own gradient — a construction that automatically
shifts weight back onto the pinball term whenever the decision-loss term grows large
relative to it. The single-price arm is therefore never trained on the decision-loss term
alone, and the modest, favourably-signed movement it shows here is attributable to that
retained calibration pressure rather than to the decision-focused term supplying an
accuracy target it structurally cannot. This also explains why the effect is not reliably
signed in either direction: nothing fixes where the combination settles beyond whatever the
pinball term alone would produce, so the result is consistent with useful training in
general rather than with a decision-focused mechanism specific to single-price settlement.

## 5.4 Transfer across settlement structures

The same argument extends to how each arm's behaviour transfers when deployed under the
settlement structure it was not trained against. If both structures reward a statistic
close to what statistical training already produces on this dataset, then which structure
supplied the training signal should matter little to the resulting decision value — and
Table 4.1 and Table 4.2 show exactly this: the dual-price arm's cross-evaluated result
under single-price settlement (−0.076%) sits close to its native dual-price result
(−0.045%), and the single-price arm's cross-evaluated result under dual-price settlement
(−0.127%) sits close to its native single-price result (−0.126%), with both directions
landing on the beneficial side of zero. This direction-independence runs counter to a
natural expectation that decision-focused training binds a forecaster tightly to the
specific task structure it was trained against, such that transplanting it into a different
settlement should degrade its value. That expectation would hold if the two structures'
decision-optimal targets were far apart; on this dataset, per §5.3, they are not, and the
transfer results follow accordingly.

## 5.5 Relation to prior decision-focused results

The muted economic effect reported here contrasts with prior decision-focused work within
and adjacent to the dispatchable feeder literature, all of which reports a measurable
decision-value gain. Beichter et al. report a 9.45% reduction in average daily total costs
from decision-focused fine-tuning of a time series foundation model
[@beichterDecisionfocusedFinetuningTime2025]; Depoortere et al. report a 3.6% reduction in
normalised electricity costs from a differentiable convex battery-scheduling formulation
structurally similar to the one used for training in this study, achieved at the cost of a
substantially worse RMSE (19.9%, against 8.2% for a statistical baseline)
[@depoortereDecisionfocusedLearningOptimal2026]; and Werling et al. report a non-monotonic
but generally favourable relationship between forecast quality and decision value within
the dispatchable feeder framework, for both point [@werlingImpactForecastCharacteristics2023]
and probabilistic [@werlingPointProbabilisticForecasts2026] forecasts, with the
highest-quality probabilistic forecast attaining the best cost in the latter case. A
structural difference between this study and all three is the cost geometry used to train
against. Beichter, Depoortere, and Werling's formulations all use an abstract, deterministic
cost function with a stationary geometry, so the gradient direction supplied to training is
consistent across every sample. Real settlement prices do not share this property: the
sign of the day-ahead/imbalance spread, and with it the direction a decision-focused
gradient pushes the forecaster, reverses from period to period as prices move. This
difference is not visible while training against an abstract cost function with fixed
geometry, and only becomes visible once that abstract cost is replaced with a real one, as
in the present study — a plausible structural reason why a positive decision-value result
that generalises across a stationary synthetic cost need not generalise to real settlement
prices without further qualification.

## 5.6 Wider implications

Independent of any specific trained model, single-price settlement's decision-loss gradient
has no minimum with respect to the forecast — a structural property of the objective,
checkable algebraically before any training takes place, not something that depends on how
any particular run turns out. The clearest evidence in this study of what that absence
actually costs is not a demonstrated failure under deployment but the seed instability
documented in §4.2 and §4.3: the single-price arm carries the widest seed-to-seed spread of
any retrained arm, both in its forecast bias and in its economic cost, because nothing in
its decision objective disciplines where a given training run settles. In this study, that
outcome is arrested only by the pinball-loss term that remains part of the combined
training objective throughout — the decision-focused term supplies no accuracy pressure of
its own, and the calibration that survives does so because a separate loss term is doing
the disciplining work. This is a governance point independent of whether harm is
demonstrated in any one evaluation: under single-price settlement, decision-focused
training produces a bidding strategy in a forecast's clothing, held to any statistical
standard only by whatever else is present in the loss function, and a practitioner
adopting this approach should verify, before training, whether their chosen settlement
structure's decision objective supplies its own accuracy-restoring minimum or not — a
property derivable directly from the settlement's cost geometry, independent of the
specific data or model involved, and consistent with single-price settlement's documented
real-market property of financially rewarding, rather than penalising, deviations that
happen to restore system balance.

In practice, this reduces to two conditions an operator can check before adopting a
decision-focused retraining pipeline for a given settlement structure, both derivable from
the cost formula alone and neither requiring a trained model to evaluate. First, does the
settlement's decision objective have a genuine minimum with respect to the forecast — is
it non-linear in a way that implies a specific, price-determined target, as dual-price
settlement does here? Second, if it does not, does the training objective actually used
retain an independent calibration-anchoring term, as the combined pinball/decision-loss
objective in this study does for its single-price arm? A settlement structure that fails
the first condition and a training objective that fails the second, together, is the
configuration in which decision-focused retraining has no mechanism left to prevent an
economically unconstrained forecast.
