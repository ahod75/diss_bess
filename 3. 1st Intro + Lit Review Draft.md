# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Renewable Grid Integration
Modern power systems have experienced a rapid proliferation of renewable energy resources, accounting for nearly half of global electricity generation capacity by the end of 2025 [@RenewablePowerGeneration2026]. However, the integration of these renewable assets into legacy power grids introduces operational and technical challenges [@ShiftEnergySubstitution]. Historically, power grids have been designed around predictable, turbine-based forms of electricity generation that provide grid stability and can be dispatched to meet power demand. Despite this, prevalent forms of renewable generation are weather-dependent, stochastic, and often distributed across medium- and low-voltage distribution networks. Power system infrastructure is struggling to deal with the changing loads introduced by renewable generation [@strezoskiIntegrationUtilityDistributed2022]. As a result, significant capital expenditure is required to accommodate new renewable capacity. According to the IEA [@ElectricityGridsSecure2023], global annual investment in electricity grids must double from roughly $300 billion in 2023 to over $600 billion by 2030. Whilst substantial grid investment is unavoidable, considerable cost savings can be achieved through the active management of generation and demand assets [@PathwaysCommercialLiftoff].

## 1.2 The Dispatchable Feeder Framework
A practical approach to realising these cost savings is by aggregating generation and demand assets by electrical grid topology, thereby allowing for the consideration of physical grid constraints. The dispatchable feeder (DF) provides an operational framework for achieving this by aggregating localised sources of inflexible generation and demand with controllable energy storage (such as a battery energy storage system, or BESS) to schedule and dispatch net load in advance [@beichterRealWorldDispatchableFeeder2023]. The framework is scale-agnostic and can be applied across multiple levels of the energy system, ranging from the control of individual households [@werlingLinerestrictedDispatchableFeeders2022] to entire distribution feeder networks [@sossanAchievingDispatchabilityDistribution2016].
As a DF's power dispatch schedule must be committed prior to real-time operation, the operational and economic performance of a DF depends heavily on the forecasting quality of its net load, referred to as 'prosumption' within DF literature. Prosumption is typically driven by weather-dependent generation and stochastic demand [@sossanAchievingDispatchabilityDistribution2016] and is inherently uncertain. Consequently, significant operational and economic value can be derived from probabilistic forecasting and risk-aware decision-making frameworks that account for this uncertainty [@appinoUseProbabilisticForecasts2018a].

## 1.3 Decision Value of Forecasting
DF power dispatch represents a specific class of decision-making under uncertainty. The standard approach for such problems is to treat forecasting and decision-making as two separate stages [@mandiDecisionFocusedLearningFoundations2024]. Under this 'predict-then-optimise' paradigm, a machine-learning (ML) model is first trained to minimise the prediction error of an uncertain variable under a specific statistical measure. The model's prediction is then passed to a separate optimiser, where it is used to make a decision based on a specific objective, such as minimising costs. This paradigm is based on the broad assumption that improving the statistical accuracy of the prediction leads to better downstream decisions. However, the decision-making value of a forecast may not always align with the specific statistical measure the forecast is trained against [@cameronPerilsLearningOptimizing2021]. Therefore, a statistically accurate forecast may yield worse downstream decisions than one that is deliberately biased in a decision-relevant way. This is often the case in modern power systems with a high penetration of renewable generation, where the costs of over-prediction and under-prediction can be asymmetric [@moralesIntegratingRenewablesElectricity2014]. Recent work has demonstrated this gap between prosumption forecast accuracy and decision value within the DF framework, finding that the best-performing forecasts are not always the most statistically accurate [@werlingImpactForecastCharacteristics2023]. The field of decision-focused learning (DFL) aims to address the gap between forecast accuracy and decision value by integrating the decision-making process directly into the training of the forecaster [@mandiDecisionFocusedLearningFoundations2024]. DFL encompasses a broad family of approaches. However, applying DFL to power systems and, consequently, to DFs, raises distinct structural and mathematical challenges [@zhangDecisionFocusedLearningPower2026]. Firstly, operational limits on dispatch decisions must be adhered to, since violations risk damaging capital-intensive infrastructure and compromising grid stability. Secondly, computational tractability must be maintained, as differentiating through a constrained optimisation problem is computationally expensive and is not always mathematically viable, particularly for formulations involving integer variables or non-continuous cost surfaces [@mandiDecisionFocusedLearningFoundations2024].

## 1.4 Research Motivation and Questions
Multiple approaches have been used to align forecasting with decision value in DF-related contexts which aim to maximise decision quality whilst maintaining computational tractability and enforcing operational constraints. Whilst these methods have shown promising results [@werlingAutomatingValueOrientedForecast2024; @werlingPointProbabilisticForecasts2026; @depoortereDecisionfocusedLearningOptimal2026; @beichterDecisionFocusedRetrainingForecast2024], there remain several critical gaps in the literature. Firstly, existing DF research has predominantly explored decision-value improvements within idealised settings, aiming to minimise abstract, deterministic cost functions. Such functions do not accurately represent the true financial penalties a DF would incur when operating within a real market environment. Furthermore, existing DF literature has yet to explore improving the decision-value of probabilistic forecasts, relying instead on deterministic point forecasts.

This dissertation aims to address these gaps. It extends the work of [@beichterDecisionFocusedRetrainingForecast2024] by performing decision-focused retraining of a probabilistic quantile forecaster, differentiating through a single-stage stochastic DF formulation similar to that used in [@depoortereDecisionfocusedLearningOptimal2026]. The trained forecasters are subsequently evaluated through a recourse-aware robust DF formulation based on [@stratigakosPrescriptiveTreesIntegrated2022]. To ensure realistic operational conditions, this study utilises historical load data spanning 2018 to 2020 from Plymouth [@singletonElectricityDemandData2021], alongside historical day-ahead and imbalance prices from the GB power market [@SystemSellBuy; @EuropeanWholesaleElectricity]. Forecasters are trained and evaluated under the GB single-price imbalance market and a stylised dual-price imbalance market formulated using the same data, accounting for realistic day-ahead gate-closure timing [@N2EXDayAhead].

Consequently, this research aims to address two primary questions:

1. How does the decision value of a decision-retrained probabilistic forecaster compare to control statistically retrained and baseline probabilistic forecaster when trained under realistic dispatchable feeder market structures, and how does that value transfer when deployed under the other settlement structure?

4. How does decision-focused retraining alter the predictive distributions of a probabilistic prosumption forecaster across different imbalance settlement structures, and do these alterations align with the theoretical cost geometry of each market?

# 2. Literature Review

## 2.1 Forecasting for the Dispatchable Feeder
DF operation is typically broken down into two stages [@beichterRealWorldDispatchableFeeder2023]. First, a forecast of prosumption is used to produce a day-ahead power dispatch schedule. This is typically done by solving an optimisation problem which computes the optimal dispatch schedule for forecasted prosumption by minimising an objective function representing a key operational value, such as total daily costs. Then, real-time operation of the DF makes up for deviations from the schedule caused by differences between forecasted and realised prosumption. Sossan et al. [@sossanAchievingDispatchabilityDistribution2016] use deterministic point forecasts of prosumption and apply model predictive control (MPC) for second-stage real-time DF operation to ensure that all DF actions adhere to physical BESS and grid operational constraints.

However, point forecasts have no way of accounting for the inherent uncertainty of future prosumption, and therefore have no way of quantifying the feasibility of a given dispatch schedule. Appino et al. [@appinoUseProbabilisticForecasts2018a] account for this by using probabilistic forecasts of prosumption and stochastic robust optimisation to determine the first-stage dispatch schedule. By combining stochastic optimisation's probabilistic treatment of uncertainty [@birgeIntroductionStochasticProgramming2011] with robust optimisation's worst-case feasibility guarantees [@lokeRobustOptimization2024], their approach ensures schedule feasibility across a defined uncertainty range while reducing total costs and schedule deviations compared to deterministic methods. To achieve this, Appino et al. [@appinoStorageSchedulingStochastic2018] establish the use of an abstract cost function to represent DF costs, a convention that is followed extensively in later DF literature. Their first-stage day-ahead cost function applies asymmetric linear and quadratic penalty terms to net positive and negative prosumption at each timestep. Their second-stage imbalance cost function symmetrically penalises absolute deviation from the day-ahead schedule with shared linear and quadratic penalty terms. Further building upon this probabilistic DF framework, Werling et al. [@werlingLinerestrictedDispatchableFeeders2022] demonstrate how probabilistic forecasts can mitigate overloading in photovoltaic-dominated low-voltage grids while avoiding generation curtailment.

Whilst these approaches showcase the benefits of addressing the uncertainty of future prosumption using probabilistic forecasting, they leave the consideration of the decision value of probabilistic forecasts unexplored.

## 2.2 The divergence between forecast quality and forecast value
Forecast quality measures a prediction's statistical accuracy from the forecaster's perspective using metrics like mean absolute error (MAE), mean squared error (MSE), or continuous ranked probability score (CRPS). In contrast, forecast value quantifies its practical utility based on the realised costs of the downstream decisions it informs [@werlingAutomatingValueOrientedForecast2024].

Werling et al. [@werlingImpactForecastCharacteristics2023] address the divergence between forecast quality and value within the DF framework by training point-based prosumption forecasts using various centre-oriented and biased quantile-based statistical loss functions. The resulting relationship between forecast quality and average daily costs was non-monotonic, demonstrating that improving statistical accuracy can sometimes lead to measurably worse DF decisions. Furthermore, the value of a given forecast varied based on problem-specific parameters, most notably battery energy capacity and realised prosumption profile, meaning no single loss function dominated across the tested DFs. This implies that the value-optimal forecast is fundamentally a property of the specific deployment rather than the forecasting problem itself. Extending this analysis to probabilistic inputs, Werling et al. [@werlingPointProbabilisticForecasts2026] found that the CRPS-to-cost relationship was also non-monotonic, with the qualification that the highest-quality forecast did attain the best value on total costs. Notably, both studies quantify forecast value using the abstract cost function established by Appino et al. [@appinoUseProbabilisticForecasts2018a], directly tying these empirical findings to standard DF formulations.

Broadly, addressing the divergence between forecast quality and value within decision-making under uncertainty can be framed as a shift from predictive to prescriptive analytics [@bertsimasPredictivePrescriptiveAnalytics2018], where the objective of learning a forecast becomes improving the quality of a related downstream decision.

Whilst incorporating decision quality directly into forecaster training, a field known as decision-focused learning (DFL) [@mandiDecisionFocusedLearningFoundations2024], has been shown to improve decision quality across a range of real-world applications [@mandiDecisionFocusedLearningFoundations2024], Cameron et al. [@cameronPerilsLearningOptimizing2021] provide a specific theoretical characterisation of when this shift is beneficial. In standard workflows where forecasting is isolated from decision-making, the forecasting model must be trained to fit a pre-selected statistical distribution. Incorporating decision value into the training loop removes this restriction, allowing the model to adaptively prioritise distributional features that minimise downstream operational costs. Cameron et al. [@cameronPerilsLearningOptimizing2021] quantify this theoretical difference, demonstrating that incorporating decision value into training and standard statistical forecaster training each achieve optimality under distinct structural conditions. Notably, statistical training outperforms DFL under linear decision objectives, whereas DFL gains the upper hand when the objective function is non-linear in the prediction target.

This indicates that the advantage of incorporating decision value into forecaster training is structural rather than universal. Consequently, this motivates evaluating decision-trained forecasters within the DF framework under differing cost structures to understand the relation between forecast quality and value under each regime.

## 2.3 Decision-focused learning and its application to the DF framework
One method for improving a forecaster's decision value is to make the decision cost differentiable with respect to the forecast, allowing for its direct incorporation in forecaster training. To this end, several methods have been developed for differentiating optimisation problems. For convex problems, this is typically achieved via implicit differentiation of the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions, first introduced for quadratic programs [@amosOptNetDifferentiableOptimization2021] and later generalised to disciplined convex programs by Agrawal et al. [@agrawalDifferentiableConvexOptimization2019]. Alternative methodologies have been developed for different problem classes. Elmachtoub and Grigas [@elmachtoubSmartPredictThen2020] use convex surrogate losses to avoid differentiation in linear programming. Further recent work has extended this framework for application within stochastic optimisation problems [@dontiTaskbasedEndtoendModel2019] and discrete optimisation, supported by dedicated software packages [@ferberMIPaaLMixedInteger2019; @huMultiStagePredict+OptimizeMixed; @tangPyEPOPyTorchbasedEndtoEnd2024].
  
Decision-focused learning has seen wide application across energy dispatch and trading. Stratigakos et al. [@stratigakosPrescriptiveTreesIntegrated2022] optimise wind and storage bidding by embedding these decisions into prescriptive tree training, utilising affine decision rules [@georghiouDecisionRuleApproach2019] for second-stage recourse actions. Yi et al. [@yiDecisionFocusedPredictthenBidFramework2025] explore the application of DFL in power storage market bidding by embedding a three-layer storage bidding optimisation problem in forecaster training, whereas Paredes et al. [@paredesParticipationEnergyStorage2025] demonstrate that DFL can increase storage trading profits in reserve markets by up to 9.5%. The paradigm has also been extended to broader grid operations, including unit commitment and unit commitment with decision-dependent uncertainty [@wuSmartPredictThenOptimizeBasedUnit2026; @xiePredictandOptimizeRobustUnit2025; @ellinasDecisionfocusedLearningDecision2024a]. 

Within the DF framework itself, however, the constraints of tractability and feasibility have led to three distinct methods for selecting or training forecasters based on downstream decision value. The first method avoids differentiation-based training altogether. To find the value-optimal forecaster for individual buildings, Werling et al. [@werlingAutomatingValueOrientedForecast2024] automate model selection using meta-learning, treating it as a classification problem. This achieves comparable results to manual selection but at a significantly reduced computational cost. Notably, Werling et al. [@werlingPointProbabilisticForecasts2026] adopted this approach specifically to avoid differentiation-based DFL, which they judged computationally expensive and overly restrictive regarding the required form of the optimisation problem. However, as forecaster selection is constrained to a pool of pre-trained point forecasters, potential improvements in forecast value are confined to choosing among fixed alternatives rather than dynamically learning the optimal predictive distribution.

The second method retains differentiation-based DFL, but replaces the optimisation problem with a learned approximation. Beichter et al. [@beichterDecisionFocusedRetrainingForecast2024] train a neural network surrogate to approximate the DF decision cost and retrain the forecaster against it. They later extend this approach to fine-tune a time series foundation model [@beichterDecisionfocusedFinetuningTime2025], reporting a 9.45% reduction in average daily total costs relative to prediction-focused fine-tuning. However, whilst this formulation reduces computational costs compared to differentiating through an optimisation problem, the decision-cost gradient produced when differentiating the surrogate carries information about a global approximation of the problem, rather than the exact costs incurred by a specific DF. 

The third method differentiates through a convex optimisation problem using the differentiable layers developed by Agrawal et al. [@agrawalDifferentiableConvexOptimization2019]. In a related context to the DF framework, Depoortere et al. [@depoortereDecisionfocusedLearningOptimal2026] use this methodology to train a point forecast of photovoltaic generation to improve decisions made in small-scale residential battery scheduling, achieving a 3.6% reduction in normalised electricity costs. This cost reduction came at reduced forecast quality, with the DFL model showing a significantly worse RMSE (19.9%) compared to a statistical baseline (8.2%). Interestingly, forecast quality was improved whilst retaining forecast value by warm-starting DFL training from the statistical model.

Crucially, all reviewed DF-specific papers use the generalised cost function first developed by Appino et al. [@appinoStorageSchedulingStochastic2018]. Because no existing DF research has investigated how a learned forecast depends on the specific market structure it was trained against, it remains unknown whether the resulting predictive behaviours are generic or market-specific. This gap further motivates the evaluation of decision-trained forecasters under differing market cost structures.

## 2.4 Imbalance market settlement structures
Whilst existing DF literature has predominantly explored power dispatch using abstract, deterministic cost functions, the operational reality of power dispatch is governed by imbalance settlement mechanisms. The choice of settlement mechanism dictates the mathematical geometry of the cost function applied to forecast errors, specifically determining whether the financial penalty for deviating from a day-ahead commitment is symmetric or asymmetric [@moralesIntegratingRenewablesElectricity2014].

Under a single-price settlement mechanism, market participants face a single price for deviating from their committed day-ahead schedule regardless of whether they are long (under-consuming or over-generating) or short (over-consuming or under-generating) of their committed position. This single-price mechanism means that imbalance costs scale linearly with imbalance, and participant deviations that help restore overall system balance can be financially rewarded.

Conversely, under a dual-price settlement mechanism, two distinct imbalance prices are applied depending on whether an individual participant's imbalance aggravated or mitigated the overall system balance. These prices ensure that market participants can never profit from deviating from their committed position, and impose a piecewise linear, asymmetric cost function around the point of zero deviation.

This non-linearity has profound implications for the 'predict-then-optimise' pipeline. Cameron et al. [@cameronPerilsLearningOptimizing2021] demonstrate empirically that DFL performs better than statistically-based forecaster training when the objective function is a non-linear function of prediction targets. Therefore, with non-linear objective functions such as minimising dispatch costs in a dual-price imbalance market, DFL has a theoretically grounded reason for performing better than statistically-based forecaster training. This has already been explored by Stratigakos et al. [@stratigakosPrescriptiveTreesIntegrated2022], who apply decision-focused forecasting to battery-coupled wind power trading under both single- and dual-price imbalance settlements. Notably, their formulation is structurally analogous to the DF, coupling stochastic generation with flexible storage against a committed day-ahead position subject to imbalance penalties. 

## 2.5 Research gap
The DF literature has established that probabilistic forecasts provide significant operational value [@werlingLinerestrictedDispatchableFeeders2022], that forecast value frequently diverges from statistical forecast quality [@werlingImpactForecastCharacteristics2023], and that the value-optimal forecast is a property of the individual deployment rather than of the forecasting problem itself [@werlingImpactForecastCharacteristics2023]. Furthermore, while various methods have been introduced to exploit this dynamic within the DF framework [@werlingAutomatingValueOrientedForecast2024; @beichterDecisionFocusedRetrainingForecast2024; @depoortereDecisionfocusedLearningOptimal2026], they have predominantly relied on point forecasts and abstract, deterministic cost functions.

Consequently, the behaviour of probabilistic DF formulations trained against the decision costs of realistic market mechanisms remains untested. The application of DFL within the DF framework for both single-price and dual-price imbalance markets represents a specific area of interest. Cameron et al. [@cameronPerilsLearningOptimizing2021] demonstrated that statistically-based forecaster training can outperform DFL when considering decision-making under uncertainty with linear objective functions, whereas DFL yields better decisions when the objective function is non-linear in the prediction target. The application of DFL to both market mechanisms allows for the further testing of these results in the context of a power dispatch problem.

To investigate these gaps in the literature, this dissertation performs decision-focused retraining of a probabilistic quantile prosumption forecaster, differentiating through a single-stage stochastic DF formulation under single-price and dual-price imbalance settlement mechanisms. The forecasters are subsequently evaluated through a recourse-aware DF formulation based on [@stratigakosPrescriptiveTreesIntegrated2022]. The following chapter details the datasets used for training and testing, the forecasting model, the scenario generation procedure, the dispatch formulations used for training and evaluation, and the specific market structures under which each forecaster is tested.
